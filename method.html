PROP EDGEMETHOD
THE BOARD ACCURACY METHOD

The whole machine in one paragraph: a collector wakes up on a schedule, pulls this week's player-prop lines from two free market feeds and this week's player projections from two free model feeds, matches them player by player and stat by stat, subtracts, and ranks the board by the size of the disagreement. No paid data, no logins, no scraped logins - every feed is an open door the publisher's own app walks through.

THE LINES

WHAT THE MARKET EXPECTS

FanDuel. The sportsbook's own web client reads anonymous JSON for every game: passing, rushing and receiving props with the real line and both prices (the -114/-114 style numbers). When the board shows FanDuel, you are looking at an actual posted number with juice attached.

PrizePicks. The pick'em app's public board: one flat line per player and stat, no odds. Useful as a second opinion on where the market sits, and it covers far more players than the sportsbook page does.

THE MODELS

WHAT THE PROJECTIONS EXPECT

Sleeper. The fantasy app's open API serves weekly stat-level projections - yards, catches, touchdowns, attempts - for every rostered player. Refreshed through the week.

ESPN. ESPN's fantasy API is anonymously readable and carries per-week projections in the same shape. The two models disagree with each other plenty; the Accuracy page tracks which one lands closer.

THE FOUR NUMBERS

LINE, ESTIMATE, GAP, HIT CHANCE

Every prop on the board carries four numbers. The line is the market's number. The estimate is what the model expects the player to do. The gap is the distance between them, in the stat's own units. The hit chance is graded on the player's own game log: how often his numbers clear that line - on the six stats where that test says the number is worth showing. It knows nothing about this week: not the matchup, not the injury report, not a role that changed on Tuesday. When it points the opposite way from the estimate, the card says so.

Your advantage means the estimate sits above the line - room the market is not pricing. Vegas advantage means the estimate sits under the line - the book's number looks safe. Neither is a command. It is a measurement of disagreement, not a prediction of the future and not betting advice. Models are wrong constantly - the Accuracy page shows exactly how wrong, in plain numbers.

Props that join cleanly to at least one model make the board. Props that don't - defenses, kickers, some deep backups - are counted in the data note and left out rather than guessed at.

THE HIT CHANCE

HOW IT'S COMPUTED, IN PLAIN ENGLISH

Take one player and one stat - say, a running back's rushing yards. Look at his own game log: every weekly total he actually put up, with recent games counting about twice as much as a game five weeks back. Blend that toward his position's league average - a back with 12 games is mostly himself, a rookie with 3 games is mostly the league. That gives his personal distribution: an average and a spread. The chance he clears a line is the share of that distribution above the line.

Then a correction. Raw chances from any model run overconfident, so each stat gets a calibration curve fitted on 2025 results: it nudges the raw chance toward what actually happened, capped at a 20-point swing, and shrunk further at any point on the curve with few results behind it. The calibration is graded honestly - fitted on weeks 1-12, scored on weeks 13-18, and only earlier games ever feed a prediction. Players with under 3 tracked games get no number at all, shown as a dash. The curves refresh every week as the new season's games get graded.

Six stats carry a number. Eight do not. A stat reaches the board only if 2025 produced at least a thousand graded props for it - enough to fit a twenty-one point curve and still have weeks left over to test it on. Rushing yards, rush attempts, receiving yards, receptions, targets and rush+rec yards clear that bar. Passing yards, completions, attempts, passing TDs, interceptions, pass+rush yards and the touchdown props do not, and on the held-out weeks the model ranked those no better than a coin flip - in a few cases worse. Those props show a dash. An earlier version of this site printed percentages there, some of them in the nineties; they were an artifact of fitting a twenty-one point curve to two hundred results, and they are gone.

What the calibration is actually calibrated to. Grading a hit chance needs the line the prop was offered at, and no free archive of 2025 sportsbook lines exists. Sleeper's weekly projection stands in for it. That proxy is not the same market: against Sleeper's 2025 rushing-yards numbers the over came in only 32% of the time, so the fitted curve maps a raw coin flip down to about a third - and it will do that to a real bookmaker's line too, even though a real line is priced near 50/50. It is the honest reason the board shows far more "Vegas advantage" than the other way. The fix is time: every week's props are graded against the real lines they were offered at, and once that record is deep enough the calibration gets refitted on it.

What "beats the baseline" means here. On the held-out weeks the calibrated model scores 0.2407 on the Brier score. The right thing to compare that to is not a coin flip and not the league-average model - it is simply always guessing the rate at which the over came in during the training weeks, which scores 0.2455. The model's real edge over that is about two percent. Small, real, and worth stating plainly rather than dressing up.

THE PLUMBING

WHERE IT LIVES

A scheduled job on GitHub pulls the feeds, computes the board, and writes it into a Supabase database. This site reads the latest snapshot straight from the database when you open it. Lines move during the week; the snapshot time is in the black bar up top, and every snapshot is kept so edges can be graded after the games.

Built on free public endpoints. Like every open gate, any of these feeds can close or change shape without notice - if a feed dies, the data note on the board will say which one.
